1. Electrical power

Voltage is electrical "push." Current is how much charge is flowing. Their product is electrical power going into the motor. More load means more current.

voltage×current→power in

2. Shaft sends energy out

The product of torque and speed is mechanical power. Stop the shaft and you have torque but no output power; free-spin it and you have speed but no output power.

torque×spin speed→power out

3. Choose a useful middle

The red power hill peaks at about half free speed, but that asks for huge current. For repeated FRC motion, aim toward the right side of the hill—often 65–85% free speed—then gear down for output torque.

cool & weak ← healthy operating zone → hot & strong
INTUITION: TORQUE

Torque is a twisting force.

Imagine a wrench on a bolt. Push with the same force farther from the bolt and it becomes easier to turn: that is more torque. Motors produce torque at their shafts; a pulley or arm turns that twist into a linear force.

torque (N·m) = force (N) × radius (m)

A 100 N load on a 1 in diameter pulley needs much less torque than the same load on a 4 in pulley.

INTUITION: SPEED & POWER

Speed says how fast. Power says how much work each second.

RPM counts rotations. A mechanism can have high torque but no speed when it is stalled, or high speed but almost no torque at free speed. Power is the useful middle: twist while moving.

power (W) = torque (N·m) × angular speed (rad/s)

Gearing changes the torque/speed trade but does not create power. It lets the Kraken spin in a useful range while the mechanism gets the motion it needs.

The planet must have (ring − sun) ÷ 2 teeth to bridge the two circles. Planetary math here assumes 85% efficiency per stage, a deliberately conservative FRC rule of thumb.

One pair

A 12-tooth pinion driving a 60-tooth gear is 5:1. Output is about five times slower and about five times twistier.

Two pairs

First pair 5:1; second pair 4:1. The output gets 5 × 4 = 20:1, not 9:1. Ratios multiply.

Efficiency

This lesson models a stacked regular gearbox at 95% efficient per stage. Three stages keep about 86% of ideal output power.

04 / larping as gears

Belts and chains are basically gears but further apart.

The same ratio drawing applies to pulleys and sprockets: the bigger driven wheel turns slowly. Switch the material and see why a timing belt, chain, and intentionally slipping belt each behave differently.

driven wheel size

Pick for the job

Timing belt: Best for high speed, medium torque applications. Not as efficient as chain, and you have to use specific sizes.

Roller chain: durable and compact for high loads. Can transfer higher torque per tooth than the timing belt, but moves irradically at high speeds.

Round belt: friction drive. It can slip like a soft clutch, which can protect a mechanism but cannot precisely position it.

reduction = driven teeth ÷ driving teeth

Use this sequence in real design:
  1. Pick the output speed from the game task.
  2. Draw forces / center of mass / roller acceleration to estimate load.
  3. At target output speed, multiply by ratio to get motor rpm.
  4. Read motor torque at that rpm. Multiply it by ratio and efficiency. Add margin.
ARM OR WRIST

Gravity pulls down

Worst case is usually horizontal. Draw from pivot to center of mass. Heavier mass or longer arm means more torque. Counterbalancing helps before adding motor power.

SHOOTER / ROLLER

High throughput requires getting back to speed after every shot.

Roller torque is often mostly during spin-up and while compressing/launching a game piece. Inertia, desired spin-up time, and game-piece force matter.

ELEVATOR

Lift force becomes torque

Weight pulls down; a belt or rope pulley converts that force into torque. Consider counterbalance, friction, and multi-stage geometry in the final answer.
